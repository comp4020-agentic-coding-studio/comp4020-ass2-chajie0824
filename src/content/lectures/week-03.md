## The piece that named the problem

Renwu magazine's 2020 feature built its case entirely from riders' own
accounts — shrinking delivery windows, an app penalising the seconds it
itself demanded a rider spend stopped at a red light, the sense of being
timed by something that would never have to justify the number. What made
the piece land wasn't one shocking fact; it was that "the system," not any
named manager or company, became the thing riders were describing as their
adversary. That's the shift this course keeps returning to: from a boss you
could argue with to a number you can't.

## What happened after it went viral

Meituan and Ele.me both responded publicly within days — Meituan added an
"extra five minutes" grace-period toggle riders could request in specific
cases; Ele.me made adjustments of its own. Read the announcements next to
the actual mechanism, though, and the gap is instructive: a public
statement is free to make, a change to the underlying rating or dispatch
model is not, and the two kinds of response can look identical in a press
release. Part of this week's job is learning to tell them apart from the
outside — the same skill the classification and reform lectures later this
semester depend on.

## Reading it as a case study, not an exposé

Not every claim in the piece carries the same evidential weight. A stated
average delivery-time reduction is the kind of number you could in
principle check against platform disclosures, patch notes or independent
reporting. A rider's account of what a penalty felt like to receive is
testimony — real and worth taking seriously, but not the kind of claim a
second source can confirm or deny. Your system-up-close essay lives or dies
on keeping that distinction straight: cite testimony as testimony, and
reserve "this is independently verifiable" for the claims that actually
are.

## Before the session

Read the assigned translation excerpt and find one claim in it you could
