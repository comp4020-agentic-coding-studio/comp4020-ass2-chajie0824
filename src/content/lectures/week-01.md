## What "algorithmic management" means

Lee, Kusbit, Metsky and Dabbish's 2015 study gave this course its working
definition: algorithmic management is what happens when software takes over
decisions a human manager used to make — who does which job, how their
performance is rated, when they get warned or let go. None of that requires
a robot boss standing over anyone. It only requires that the deciding moved
from a person into a rule nobody in the room can point to.

Food delivery is the cleanest place on the planet to watch this happen,
because almost the entire job — which order you get, how long you're told
it should take, whether you're rated well enough to keep getting more work
— is decided by software before a human supervisor ever enters the picture.
That's why this course keeps returning to delivery work as its object,
whichever platform or country a given week is looking at.

## How the semester is shaped

Two halves. Weeks 2–6 open up the machinery itself — dispatch and ETA
prediction, rating and lateness thresholds, the resistance riders build
back, and the economics that make labour the one line item a platform
treats as flexible — anchored in a single deep case study: China's
food-delivery sector, read through Renwu magazine's 2020 feature "Delivery
Riders, Trapped in the System," which gave this course its title. Weeks
7–12 turn the same mechanisms over to regulators, comparing how China,
Australia and the EU each answer the same question differently: what is an
algorithm allowed to decide about a worker's day.

## The claim we're testing

"The algorithm decided" is a sentence you'll hear from every platform this
semester touches, and it can mean two very different things. Sometimes it's
an accurate description of a system genuinely too complex for any one
person to have specified the outcome by hand. Sometimes it's a place where
a decision was made by a specific person, on a specific date, and the
sentence exists to make the paper trail stop there. Telling the two apart —
not assuming either one by default — is the actual skill this course is
trying to build, and it's the standard every essay this semester is held
to.
