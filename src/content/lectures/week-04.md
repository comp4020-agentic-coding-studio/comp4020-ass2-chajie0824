## A taxonomy, not a vibe

Lee, Kusbit, Metsky and Dabbish split algorithmic management into three
functions — directing (assigning the work), evaluating (rating it), and
disciplining (acting on the rating) — and the split matters because most
critiques collapse all three into a single word, "surveillance," that ends
up meaning everything and explaining nothing. A dispatch algorithm that
routes you to a pickup is directing. A star rating is evaluating. A
suspended account is disciplining. Naming which one a specific mechanism is
doing is the first move in arguing about it credibly.

## Ratings as discipline nobody signed up to deliver

Rosenblat and Stark's account of ride-hailing ratings generalises directly
to delivery: the customer supplies the number, the platform enforces the
consequence, and no single actor — not the customer, who thought they were
just answering a prompt, and not the platform, which is "just following the
data" — has to own the decision to discipline anyone. That diffusion of
responsibility isn't an accident of the design; it's most of what the
design is for.

## Gamification: the harder case to argue against

Streak bonuses, badge tiers, and "you're so close to your goal" nudges work
by a different mechanism entirely — recruiting a rider's own motivation
rather than monitoring their compliance. It's harder to build a case
against a bonus than against a penalty, because nobody is visibly being
punished; they're being invited, repeatedly, to want the outcome the
platform wants. Weeks 8–10 come back to exactly this asymmetry: how much
harder incentive design is to regulate than a formal disciplinary
mechanism.
