## What "algorithmic management" means

Lee, Kusbit, Metsky and Dabbish's 2015 study, "Working with Machines: The
Impact of Algorithmic and Data-Driven Management on Human Workers"
(presented at CHI, the premier human-computer interaction venue), gave
this course its working definition: algorithmic management is what happens
when software takes over decisions a human manager used to make — who
does which job, how their performance is rated, when they get warned or
let go. None of that requires a robot boss standing over anyone. It only
requires that the deciding moved from a person into a rule nobody in the
room can point to.

That definition sat mostly inside academic HCI for a few years before it
became a mainstream policy term. Kellogg, Valentine and Christin's 2020
review, "Algorithms at Work: The New Contested Terrain of Control"
(Academy of Management Annals), is the piece that best maps how far the
idea travelled — from a description of how gig platforms behaved into a
general theory of workplace control that now gets cited in labour
economics, organisational sociology and the regulatory determinations
you'll read in the second half of this course. Knowing that lineage
matters for your essays: when you cite "algorithmic management," you're
invoking a specific, traceable body of work, not a piece of jargon anyone
coined to sound serious.

Food delivery is the cleanest place on the planet to watch this happen,
because almost the entire job — which order you get, how long you're told
it should take, whether you're rated well enough to keep getting more work
— is decided by software before a human supervisor ever enters the
picture. A warehouse picker or a call-centre agent doing algorithmically
managed work still usually has a shift supervisor to appeal to when a
metric looks wrong. A delivery rider frequently does not: the app is the
only interface between the rider and the decision, start to finish. That's
why this course keeps returning to delivery work as its object, whichever
platform or country a given week is looking at.

## How the semester is shaped

Two halves. Weeks 2–6 open up the machinery itself — dispatch and ETA
prediction, rating and lateness thresholds, the resistance riders build
back, and the economics that make labour the one line item a platform
treats as flexible — anchored in a single deep case study: China's
food-delivery sector, read through Renwu magazine's 2020 feature "Delivery
Riders, Trapped in the System," which gave this course its title. Weeks
7–12 turn the same mechanisms over to regulators, comparing how China,
Australia and the EU each answer the same question differently: what is
an algorithm allowed to decide about a worker's day.

The two halves are designed to depend on each other. An essay in week 11
that compares regulatory regimes without a concrete mechanism to point to
is just describing three press releases; a week 3 close reading of a case
study without weeks 7–10's legal vocabulary can describe what happened but
not why the platforms' response took the specific legal shape it did. You
need both halves to do either one well.

## The claim we're testing

"The algorithm decided" is a sentence you'll hear from every platform this
semester touches, and it can mean two very different things. Sometimes
it's an accurate description of a system genuinely too complex for any one
person to have specified the outcome by hand. Sometimes it's a place where
a decision was made by a specific person, on a specific date, and the
sentence exists to make the paper trail stop there. Telling the two apart
— not assuming either one by default — is the actual skill this course is
trying to build, and it's the standard every essay this semester is held
to.
