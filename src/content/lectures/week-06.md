## What a subsidy war actually buys

The Meituan–Ele.me subsidy years spent enormous sums making orders
artificially cheap for customers and artificially rewarding for restaurants
to list — not because either company expected loyalty in return, but
because market share, once captured, is expensive for a rival to win back.
The spending stopped when the market had consolidated enough that another
dollar of subsidy returned less than finally trying to make money, not when
either company became profitable.

## One order, laid out in full

Delivery fee, restaurant commission, customer discount, rider pay — put
side by side for a single order, it becomes visible which party actually
absorbs the cost once a price war's subsidies are withdrawn. That's the
week-6-checkpoint exercise: build that breakdown yourself, from a published
rate card, rather than taking anyone's summary of "who pays" on faith.

## Why rider pay is the flexible line item

A restaurant's commission rate is set by contract and hard to move without
losing the restaurant. A customer-facing price is constrained by what a
competitor charges. Rider pay per delivery is neither — nothing structurally
stops a platform adjusting it order by order, region by region, in response
to supply. That's not malice; it's just the one variable in the whole
equation without an external party contractually defending it, which is
exactly why it absorbs the pressure the other three line items are
protected from.
