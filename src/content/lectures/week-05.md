## Individual workarounds and the patch that follows

Fake-GPS apps that report a rider further along their route than they
actually are; deliberately declining low-value orders to protect an
acceptance-rate score, even though the platform can read that refusal as
bad behaviour — every individual tactic riders build starts an arms race
with the next platform update. That race is itself data: a workaround only
gets patched once it's common enough to threaten a metric the platform
cares about, so which tactics survive tells you which numbers actually
matter to the business.

## Collective tactics change the shape of the risk

Regional WeChat and Telegram groups that share which restaurants are
running long kitchen delays are, functionally, riders crowd-sourcing the
information the platform's own ETA model doesn't have — and sharing it
faster than the platform can. This is a materially different kind of
resistance from an individual workaround: it's coordinated, it's visible if
anyone looks for it, and unlike a single rider spoofing their GPS, it can't
be patched by changing one parameter in a model.

## Why the platform's response tells you what it's afraid of

Individual workarounds tend to get patched quietly, in a routine update
nobody announces. Collective coordination gets escalated — flagged
internally, sometimes cited in exactly the kind of policy debate this
course studies in its second half. That asymmetry in response is itself
evidence: it tells you which kind of resistance the platform actually
treats as a threat to its model, versus which kind is just noise to route
around.
