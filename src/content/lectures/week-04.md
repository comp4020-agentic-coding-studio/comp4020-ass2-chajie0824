## A taxonomy, not a vibe

Lee, Kusbit, Metsky and Dabbish split algorithmic management into three
functions — directing (assigning the work), evaluating (rating it), and
disciplining (acting on the rating) — and the split matters because most
critiques collapse all three into a single word, "surveillance," that ends
up meaning everything and explaining nothing. A dispatch algorithm that
routes you to a pickup is directing. A star rating is evaluating. A
suspended account is disciplining. Naming which one a specific mechanism is
doing is the first move in arguing about it credibly.

## Ratings as discipline nobody signed up to deliver

Rosenblat and Stark's 2016 paper, "Algorithmic Labor and Information
Asymmetries: A Case Study of Uber's Drivers" (International Journal of
Communication), is the canonical version of this argument and it
generalises directly to delivery: the customer supplies the number, the
platform enforces the consequence, and no single actor — not the customer,
who thought they were just answering a prompt, and not the platform, which
is "just following the data" — has to own the decision to discipline
anyone. That diffusion of responsibility isn't an accident of the design;
it's most of what the design is for. Rosenblat and Stark's central
contribution is naming the asymmetry underneath it: the platform can see
every driver's full history and every passenger's rating pattern, while
each individual worker can see almost none of the data that determines
their own outcome. Delivery platforms reproduce that same asymmetry almost
exactly — a rider can see their own score, but not the distribution it's
being compared against, nor which specific deliveries moved it.

## Gamification: the harder case to argue against

Streak bonuses, badge tiers, and "you're so close to your goal" nudges work
by a different mechanism entirely — recruiting a rider's own motivation
rather than monitoring their compliance. It's harder to build a case
against a bonus than against a penalty, because nobody is visibly being
punished; they're being invited, repeatedly, to want the outcome the
platform wants. A streak bonus that resets to zero the moment a rider takes
a day off is functionally a penalty wearing a reward's framing — the
missed income is identical either way, but only one framing shows up in a
platform's marketing as a benefit. Weeks 8–10 come back to exactly this
asymmetry: how much harder incentive design is to regulate than a formal
disciplinary mechanism, because a regulator can point to a suspension and
ask a platform to justify it, but there's no equivalent hearing for a
bonus structure that quietly narrowed someone's real choices.
